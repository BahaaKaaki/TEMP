# Benchmarking / peer comparison / gap analysis

## When to use this skill
Use when the ask is for peer comparison, benchmark analysis, best-practice review, gap analysis, or lessons learned to inform a strategy, operating model, or policy choice. It is the right skill when relative position matters and the client needs to know how it compares. Do not use for a pure market scan or for a baseline with no external comparison.

## Executive summary
**One page only.**  
It should track the benchmark and usually contain 3–5 components such as the peer logic, key findings, major gaps, best-practice lessons, and what they mean for the client.

## Typical output
Typical length: 6–12 pages in a standard version. This is the default structure for a broad ask; narrower requests may focus on one benchmark dimension or one decision question only.

### 0) Executive summary — 1 pages
**What it is:** One-page tracker for the benchmark  
**What it includes:** peer logic, key findings, major gaps, lessons, implications  
**Typical formats:** message page; benchmark summary

### 1) Benchmark objective and peer selection — 1 pages
**What it is:** What the benchmark is trying to answer and why these peers were chosen  
**What it includes:** decision question, peer criteria, peer groups, comparison logic  
**Typical formats:** peer selection matrix; peer map

### 2) Comparison dimensions — 1 pages
**What it is:** The criteria used for the benchmark  
**What it includes:** dimensions, definitions, normalization logic, scope notes  
**Typical formats:** criteria table; assessment framework

### 3) Peer snapshots and comparative view — 2–4 pages
**What it is:** How the client and peers compare  
**What it includes:** peer facts, comparative scores or metrics, standout differences  
**Typical formats:** heatmap; bar chart; peer cards; spider chart

### 4) Lessons learned and best practices — 1–2 pages
**What it is:** What can be learned from leading players  
**What it includes:** best-practice themes, design patterns, transferable insights  
**Typical formats:** lesson cards; leading-practice page

### 5) Gap analysis and implications — 1–2 pages
**What it is:** What the benchmark means for the client  
**What it includes:** current-to-peer gaps, priority implications, areas to close, decisions to make  
**Typical formats:** gap bridge; priority matrix; implication page

## How to build
- start by defining the benchmark objective and the decision it should inform
- select peers with explicit rationale rather than convenience
- compare on a limited set of meaningful dimensions and normalize where needed
- use summaries first, then deeper cuts where the differences matter
- translate peer differences into lessons learned and client-specific gaps
- avoid benchmarking for its own sake by landing the so-what clearly
- end with implications, recommended moves, or design choices

## Success criteria
The peers make sense, the comparison is apples-to-apples, the biggest differences are visible quickly, and the output converts comparison into clear lessons and implications.

## Common failure modes
Peer set is arbitrary; too many dimensions; false precision; no normalization; findings buried in tables; no lessons learned; gap analysis disconnected from the client’s actual decisions.
