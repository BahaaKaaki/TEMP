# Biotech / life sciences cluster strategy and feasibility

## When to use this skill
Use this when the client is designing a biotech, life sciences, or research-and-innovation cluster and needs an integrated answer spanning sector focus, ecosystem design, real estate, funding, governance, and activation. Typical trigger phrases include “biotech cluster,” “life sciences hub,” “R&I campus,” “cluster feasibility,” and “masterplan competition.” Do **not** use it for a corporate strategy for a single pharma or healthcare entity, or for a narrow science-park real-estate study with no ecosystem ambition.

## Deliverable shape
1. **Executive summary and cluster thesis**  
   Purpose: state why the cluster should exist, what it will specialize in, and what success requires.  
   Typical length: 2–4 slides.  
   Complete when: the cluster thesis, specialization logic, economic role, and implementation implication are visible.  
   Common failure modes: treating biotech as a generic innovation district, no specialization choice, no realism on ecosystem requirements.

2. **Strategic rationale and baseline**  
   Purpose: establish the economic and policy case for the cluster.  
   Typical length: 3–5 slides.  
   Complete when: sector size, current ecosystem gaps, national priorities, and local strengths are explicit.  
   Common failure modes: science-policy rhetoric with no evidence, no local baseline, no supply-chain/security rationale.

3. **Global trends, funding models, and benchmark clusters**  
   Purpose: learn from how successful clusters evolved and were funded.  
   Typical length: 4–6 slides.  
   Complete when: relevant cluster archetypes, maturity pathways, and ecosystem enablers are synthesized.  
   Common failure modes: benchmark beauty parade, no distinction between organic and government-seeded clusters, no funding insight.

4. **Focus-area selection and opportunity sizing**  
   Purpose: choose where the cluster should concentrate first.  
   Typical length: 2–4 slides.  
   Complete when: priority domains (e.g., genomics, cell therapy, agri-biotech, diagnostics) are screened and sized with clear criteria.  
   Common failure modes: trying to cover all biotech domains at once, no screening logic, no alignment to local capabilities.

5. **Vision, positioning, and value proposition**  
   Purpose: define what kind of cluster this is and why firms, researchers, and investors will come.  
   Typical length: 2–3 slides.  
   Complete when: target users, anchor tenants, cluster role, and differentiators are explicit.  
   Common failure modes: generic “hub of innovation” language, no tenant logic, no explanation of why this cluster beats alternatives.

6. **Ecosystem design and program model**  
   Purpose: specify the non-real-estate elements of cluster success.  
   Typical length: 3–5 slides.  
   Complete when: universities, hospitals, startups, accelerators, VC/funders, global firms, community programs, and cluster-management services are designed.  
   Common failure modes: pure land-use plan, no cluster manager, no service layer, no investor/tenant support model.

7. **Real estate and infrastructure requirements**  
   Purpose: translate the thesis into space, labs, amenities, and living-lab infrastructure.  
   Typical length: 3–5 slides.  
   Complete when: labs, offices, maker space, pilot/manufacturing space, mixed-use amenities, mobility, and digital infrastructure are specified at the right level.  
   Common failure modes: standard office-park thinking, no wet/dry lab distinction, no talent-attraction amenities.

8. **Institutional, legal, regulatory, and talent enablers**  
   Purpose: define what must be true beyond the physical site.  
   Typical length: 3–5 slides.  
   Complete when: legal framework, incentives, IP and clinical regulation, entity setup, talent pipeline, and scholarship / TTO / capability-building logic are addressed.  
   Common failure modes: assuming the cluster can work without regulatory and talent reform, no entity model.

9. **Financial feasibility, funding, and investment promotion**  
   Purpose: show how the cluster is financed and de-risked.  
   Typical length: 3–5 slides.  
   Complete when: capex, opex, phasing, revenue logic, funding sources, and investor-promotion path are visible.  
   Common failure modes: no phasing, no hybrid funding logic, no investor strategy.

10. **Implementation roadmap and procurement path**  
Purpose: translate the concept into a staged activation plan.  
Typical length: 2–4 slides.  
Complete when: early quick wins, entity setup, incentives, masterplanning competition, anchor-tenant engagement, and 10-year action plan are sequenced.  
Common failure modes: roadmap starts after construction, no early ecosystem activation, no procurement / competition logic.

## Visual framework library
- **Baseline — four-enabler assessment**: regulation, funding, market/industry, and R&D capability. Use this as an outside-in way to diagnose cluster readiness.
- **Benchmarking — cluster maturity ladder**: embryonic to world-class, with governance, funding, infrastructure, and programming markers. Use instead of comparing clusters only by size.
- **Focus-area selection — screening matrix**: strategic fit × economic attractiveness × feasibility × local adjacency. Use to justify the initial specialization.
- **Ecosystem — anchor tenant and stakeholder map**: universities, hospitals, multinationals, startups, accelerators, VCs, regulators, and service providers.
- **Real estate — asset mix and lab typology matrix**: wet labs, dry labs, offices, pilot manufacturing, collaboration space, accommodation, retail, community services.
- **Funding — hybrid revenue / funding model**: public contribution, rental/real estate income, equity participation, grants, and service revenues.
- **Promotion — investor and tenant funnel**: target segments, value proposition, outreach channels, and anchor-tenant sequencing.
- **Roadmap — pilot / commercialize / innovate phases**: useful when the cluster is expected to evolve from a narrow nucleus into a broader platform over 5–10 years.

## House conventions
- **Action-title format**: titles should combine specialization and activation, e.g., “A genomics-led cluster with a professional cluster manager and phased lab build-out is the most credible entry point.”
- **Executive summary structure**: why this cluster, what specialization, what enablers, what funding logic, and what first moves.
- **How findings are articulated**: ecosystem fact → competitive implication → design consequence for focus area, infrastructure, or governance.
- **Tone**: ambitious but grounded. Biotech strategies are often over-aspirational; the pack should show realism on readiness and phasing.
- **Vocabulary**: “cluster manager,” “anchor tenants,” “living lab,” “specialization,” “technology transfer,” “pilot to commercialization,” “incentive stack,” “ecosystem services.”
- **Story discipline**: keep ecosystem design and real-estate design integrated, but distinct.

## Inputs the skill needs
- **Policy and sponsor context** (**Required**): national and local strategy, sponsor mandate, and why the cluster is being considered.
- **Local baseline** (**Required**): current biotech players, universities, hospitals, incubators, funding, regulation, and talent pipeline.
- **Benchmark inputs** (**Required**): at least 3–5 relevant clusters or cluster archetypes.
- **Technical and real-estate inputs** (**Optional but high value**): site options, infra constraints, land availability, mobility, utilities.
- **Financial assumptions** (**Required**): capex ranges, operating support assumptions, revenue logic, incentive envelopes where possible.
- **Regulatory / talent constraints** (**Required**): IP, clinical, visas, TTOs, scholarships, workforce availability.
- **Preferred focus areas / hypotheses** (**Optional**): sponsor preferences that need testing, not accepting.

## Methodology anchors
- Choose a first specialization; world-class clusters may be broad, but greenfield clusters almost always start narrow.
- Treat the cluster manager as a core design choice, not an implementation afterthought.
- Combine hard infrastructure with soft services; tenants choose ecosystems, not just buildings.
- Separate cluster feasibility from real-estate economics alone; policy, regulation, funding, and talent are binding constraints.
- Use phased development logic—pilot nucleus, early commercialization, then broader scaling.
- Include investment promotion and tenant attraction explicitly; a cluster without a demand-side commercialization plan remains theoretical.

## Done-state checklist
- The cluster has a defensible first specialization and does not try to be all of biotech.
- The ecosystem design goes beyond buildings and includes programming, services, and funding.
- The entity / governance model is visible.
- Talent and regulatory enablers are explicit.
- Real-estate requirements reflect biotech realities, not generic mixed-use assumptions.
- Financials are phased and tied to a funding approach.
- Investor and tenant attraction logic exists.
- The roadmap starts before construction completion.
- The summary can answer “Why here?” and “Why now?” convincingly.
- The deck makes clear what must happen in the first 24 months, not just in year 10.

## Composition notes
This skill often composes with **Strategic business case and feasibility study**, **Holding company / subsidiary governance and incorporation plan**, and **Final client readout / board decision deck**. Use **Destination development strategy and business plan** only if the project is fundamentally place-led and biotech is secondary rather than the primary economic thesis.

## Metadata
- **Primary sectors:** biotech, life sciences, RDI, public sector, real estate  
- **Typical engagement duration:** 12–38 weeks depending on stage  
- **Typical team size:** 5–10 consultants plus sector / real-estate / legal SMEs  
- **Primary consumer seniority:** manager / partner  
- **Decay risk:** medium  
- **Suggested owner discipline:** sector strategy and innovation clusters
