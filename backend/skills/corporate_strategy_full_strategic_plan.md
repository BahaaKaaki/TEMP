# Corporate strategy – full strategic plan

## When to use this skill

Use this when the client needs an entity-level strategy refresh, a new 3–5 year strategic plan, or a board-ready articulation of where to play, how to win, and how to execute. Typical trigger phrases include “strategy refresh,” “2028/2030 strategy,” “strategic plan,” “growth strategy,” and “strategic pillars.” Do **not** use it for an ecosystem-wide sector strategy or for a greenfield investment case where the core question is viability rather than enterprise direction; those are better handled by the sector playbook or business case skills.

## Deliverable shape

1. **Executive summary and recommendation**
  Purpose: land the answer before the process.  
   Typical length: 2–4 slides.  
   Complete when: it states the strategic thesis, 4–6 pillars, value/impact ambition, major trade-offs, and the first decisions required.  
   Common failure modes: process-heavy summary, generic pillars, no quantified ambition, no clear recommendation.
2. **Context, mandate, and case for change**
  Purpose: anchor why the strategy is being refreshed and what the strategy must solve.  
   Typical length: 1–2 slides.  
   Complete when: external context, mandate boundaries, ownership constraints, and parent/national alignment are explicit.  
   Common failure modes: too much macro context, weak linkage to the client’s actual decisions, failure to define in/out of mandate.
3. **Current-state diagnostic**
  Purpose: show the fact base on performance, portfolio, capabilities, and structural issues.  
   Typical length: 3–5 slides.  
   Complete when: the diagnostic covers economics, customer/market position, operating weaknesses, capability gaps, and unresolved strategic tensions.  
   Common failure modes: data dump, descriptive SWOT without hierarchy, performance charts with no implication.
4. **Outside-in market and benchmark view**
  Purpose: frame where the market is moving and what a credible strategy must respond to.  
   Typical length: 3–5 slides.  
   Complete when: peer and adjacent benchmarks, structural trends, and scenario assumptions are tied directly to the client’s choices.  
   Common failure modes: benchmarks that are not comparable, trends with no consequence, local-only peer set.
5. **Strategic choices / where to play**
  Purpose: make the choice architecture visible.  
   Typical length: 2–4 slides.  
   Complete when: the deck shows explicit choices by business, segment, geography, channel, capability, or role in the value chain, with options considered and filtered.  
   Common failure modes: turning choices into broad aspirations, listing every possible growth lever, not showing what the client will stop or deprioritize.
6. **Strategic positioning and value proposition**
  Purpose: crystallize the winning posture.  
   Typical length: 1–2 slides.  
   Complete when: the role in the market/ecosystem, distinctive value proposition, and differentiating capabilities are clear.  
   Common failure modes: vague positioning language, buzzwords, or duplication of vision/mission.
7. **Strategy house: vision, mission, pillars, objectives**
  Purpose: translate choices into a stable strategic architecture.  
   Typical length: 2–3 slides.  
   Complete when: 4–6 pillars, a small number of objectives under each, and a clean cascade from vision to objectives are visible.  
   Common failure modes: too many pillars, overlap across pillars, objectives phrased as activities rather than outcomes.
8. **Initiative portfolio and flagship moves**
  Purpose: turn the strategy into a portfolio of concrete actions.  
   Typical length: 4–8 slides.  
   Complete when: major initiatives are grouped by pillar, sized qualitatively or quantitatively, sequenced, and assigned to owners or functions.  
   Common failure modes: laundry-list initiatives, no prioritization, no links back to pillars, no quick wins versus structural moves distinction.
9. **Financial implications and KPI cascade**
  Purpose: show what success means in numbers.  
   Typical length: 2–4 slides.  
   Complete when: headline growth/cost/service targets, KPI logic, and performance ownership are visible.  
   Common failure modes: only financial targets, no operational KPIs, no bridge from strategy to numbers.
10. **Implementation roadmap and governance**

Purpose: show how the plan will be executed and protected.  
Typical length: 2–4 slides.  
Complete when: the roadmap is phased, key dependencies and decision gates are clear, and the governance model explains how progress will be challenged.  
Common failure modes: generic roadmap, no decision rights, no PMO/EMO logic, no ownership for cross-functional initiatives.

1. **Risks, dependencies, and immediate next steps**

Purpose: de-risk approval and launch.  
Typical length: 1–2 slides.  
Complete when: the few issues that could derail the strategy are explicit, with mitigations and near-term actions.  
Common failure modes: cosmetic risk lists, no real dependencies, next steps detached from approval reality.

## Visual framework library

- **Executive summary — answer-first storyboard**: 3–5 headline boxes or tiles covering context, diagnosis, recommendation, impact, and next steps. Use instead of a narrative paragraph wall when the audience is board or ExCom level.
- **Context / mandate — strategic tension page**: 2-column layout showing “what the client must deliver” versus “what is constraining it.” Use when mandate or ownership structure materially limits choice.
- **Diagnostic — benchmark bar chart with quartile bands**: peer and adjacent peers across 5–7 KPIs, with the client clearly marked. Use when performance gap needs to be explicit; use a heat map only when metrics are too heterogeneous.
- **Outside-in — trend-to-implication cascade**: macro or market trends on the left, client-specific implications on the right. Use instead of generic trend pages.
- **Choices — attractiveness × right-to-win matrix**: 2x2 for businesses, segments, or plays. Use when prioritization matters; use a scorecard instead when there are many criteria or governance sensitivities.
- **Positioning — strategy house**: vision and mission at top, 4–6 pillars, 2–4 objectives per pillar, enablers at base. This is the house style for final articulation.
- **Initiatives — initiative tree**: pillar → initiative family → flagship initiative. Use when there are many initiatives and hierarchy matters; use charters only for the top 10–15.
- **Financials — ambition bridge / waterfall**: current state → structural gap → strategy contribution → target state. Use when leadership will ask how the target is built.
- **KPI cascade — KPI tree**: strategic pillar to objective to KPI to owner. Use when the client has weak performance management maturity.
- **Roadmap — phased initiative roadmap**: lanes by workstream or pillar, with milestones, dependencies, and governance forums. Use a Gantt only if dates matter; otherwise use waves (0–6m, 6–18m, 18m+).

## House conventions

- **Action-title format**: declarative and specific. Good titles read like “DTC can unlock the next growth curve through three themes—organic, inorganic, and digital—supported by five enabling moves,” not “Strategic themes.”
- **Executive summary structure**: 2–4 slides for major strategies. Slide 1 gives the answer. Slides 2–4 cover why now, what changes, and how value is protected.
- **How findings are articulated**: evidence → insight → implication → recommendation. If the implication is missing, the page is not finished.
- **Tone**: crisp, directional, and low-hedge where the evidence is strong. Strategy pages should read as choices, not brainstorms.
- **Vocabulary**: “where to play,” “how to win,” “differentiating capabilities,” “strategic pillars,” “initiative portfolio,” “strategic enablers,” “roadmap,” “KPI cascade,” and “governance model.”
- **Page economy**: the core deck should hold the narrative. Method detail, data tables, and benchmark backup belong in appendix, not in the main flow.

## Inputs the skill needs

- **Client context** (**Required**): sector, geography, scale, ownership structure, and the current mandate.
- **Existing strategy materials** (**Optional but high value**): previous plans, board decks, annual reports, and KPI packs.
- **Performance data** (**Required**): revenues/costs/volumes, customer metrics, operational metrics, or at least directional performance indicators.
- **Market inputs** (**Required**): market growth, competitive set, benchmark data, and relevant policy context.
- **Known hypotheses / partner POV** (**Optional**): where the leadership team already leans.
- **Constraints** (**Required**): capital, regulatory constraints, political considerations, capability limits, or parent-entity requirements.
- **Decision calendar** (**Optional but high value**): board, committee, or budget deadlines.
- **Appendix tolerance** (**Optional**): whether the audience wants a lean story deck or a heavy evidence pack.

## Methodology anchors

- Select peers with purpose: use direct peers for performance calibration and adjacent players for strategic imagination; do not rely on one peer set for both.
- Keep the strategic architecture tight: 4–6 pillars is usually the upper bound before overlap and repetition start.
- Separate outcomes from activity: “improve monetization of X” is an objective; “launch a new team” is an initiative.
- Force choice visibility: if everything remains in scope, the strategy is not done.
- Build targets from bridges, not slogans: show how the ambition is constructed and what assumptions it depends on.
- Distinguish structural moves from quick wins: leadership needs both, but they should not be mixed on the same page.
- Governance is part of the strategy: if cross-functional delivery is material, the implementation office and challenge cadence belong in the main deck, not as afterthoughts.

## Done-state checklist

- The recommendation is visible on page 1 and can be repeated in one sentence by a partner without reopening the deck.
- The deck shows explicit strategic choices, not just ambitions.
- The outside-in view materially changes the answer; it is not a decorative benchmark section.
- Each pillar has a limited number of objectives and a credible initiative set beneath it.
- Quantified targets are consistent with the initiative portfolio and timing.
- The roadmap has owners, waves, and dependencies.
- Risks are real and decision-relevant, not generic.
- The appendix exists, but the main story stands on its own.
- Titles are declarative and specific throughout.
- The deck answers the inevitable challenge: “Why this strategy and not a different one?”

## Composition notes

This skill commonly composes with **Target operating model – design and activation blueprint**, **Weekly SteerCo / PMO status deck**, and **Final client readout / board decision deck**. Use it first when the strategic answer is unsettled; invoke the TOM skill when the strategy is agreed and the question shifts to how the organization should run.
