# Destination development strategy and business plan

## When to use this skill
Use this when the client is shaping a tourism, leisure, culture, nature, or mixed-use destination and needs a coherent answer across positioning, demand, asset mix, governance, financials, and implementation. Typical trigger phrases include “destination strategy,” “asset mix,” “tourism concept,” “masterplan brief,” “park / reserve / mountain destination,” “DevCo / DMO,” and “operator strategy.” Do **not** use it for a national tourism sector strategy or a single-asset feasibility study where the destination system is not in scope.

## Deliverable shape
1. **Executive summary and destination thesis**  
   Purpose: state the destination role, target demand, and the recommended development logic.  
   Typical length: 2–4 slides.  
   Complete when: the summary covers positioning, flagship assets, phasing logic, financial direction, and immediate decisions.  
   Common failure modes: aspirational place-branding with no development logic, no distinction between attraction and viability.

2. **Site context and strategic role**  
   Purpose: explain why this destination matters and what role it plays in the wider region or national agenda.  
   Typical length: 2–3 slides.  
   Complete when: location, accessibility, natural/cultural differentiators, ownership/land context, and strategic role are clear.  
   Common failure modes: site page becomes a photo montage, no role in wider tourism system, hidden access constraints.

3. **Market sensing and benchmark destinations**  
   Purpose: calibrate what kinds of destinations succeed and what archetypes are relevant.  
   Typical length: 3–5 slides.  
   Complete when: benchmark destinations are grouped by archetype and linked to the site’s opportunity set.  
   Common failure modes: random benchmark tourism slides, no implication for asset mix or visitor segments.

4. **Demand assessment and target segments**  
   Purpose: identify who will come, when, for what, and at what spending/paying power level.  
   Typical length: 3–5 slides.  
   Complete when: demand is segmented by source market / trip purpose / season / income / audience type and translated into visitor assumptions.  
   Common failure modes: top-down demand only, no seasonality, no segment prioritization, no validation with market data or surveys.

5. **Positioning and experience proposition**  
   Purpose: articulate what the destination is and is not.  
   Typical length: 2–3 slides.  
   Complete when: the positioning, experience pillars, and differentiating storyline are explicit and reflected in target segments.  
   Common failure modes: trying to serve everyone, generic luxury / family / wellness mix, no experience hierarchy.

6. **Development program, asset mix, and phasing**  
   Purpose: translate strategy into tangible assets over time.  
   Typical length: 4–6 slides.  
   Complete when: anchor assets, hospitality, residential, retail, community facilities, mobility, and public realm are specified with phasing logic.  
   Common failure modes: asset mix disconnected from demand, too much capex in wave one, no distinction between anchors and support uses.

7. **Operating model, governance, and operator / partner strategy**  
   Purpose: show who will develop, activate, and run the destination.  
   Typical length: 2–4 slides.  
   Complete when: DevCo/DMO/operator roles, partnership models, and governance mechanisms are defined.  
   Common failure modes: assuming the client can do everything, no role clarity between asset owner and operator, no investor logic.

8. **Financial feasibility and funding strategy**  
   Purpose: prove the destination can be funded and can improve over time.  
   Typical length: 3–5 slides.  
   Complete when: capex, opex, revenue structure, IRR / NPV / peak funding, and alternative development/funding plays are assessed.  
   Common failure modes: no scenario analysis, no phasing sensitivity, no funding strategy beyond “public investment.”

9. **Socio-economic, environmental, and stakeholder implications**  
   Purpose: quantify broader value and manage constraints.  
   Typical length: 1–3 slides.  
   Complete when: jobs, GDP, local community effects, environmental constraints, and stakeholder implications are visible.  
   Common failure modes: ESG language with no design consequence, no local-community lens, environmental risk buried in appendix.

10. **Implementation roadmap and procurement / masterplanning path**  
Purpose: show how the destination gets from strategy to delivery.  
Typical length: 2–4 slides.  
Complete when: immediate studies, procurement, masterplanning competition, permitting, investor/operator engagement, and first-wave activation are sequenced.  
Common failure modes: roadmap starts after masterplanning, no operator/investor track, no enabling-infrastructure logic.

## Visual framework library
- **Context — site opportunity map**: location, accessibility, surrounding demand nodes, environmental / cultural constraints. Use when location is itself part of the thesis.
- **Benchmarking — destination archetype gallery**: cluster benchmarks by mountain, reserve, urban regeneration, island, heritage, wellness, etc. Use instead of a long benchmark list.
- **Demand — visitor segmentation pyramid**: primary, secondary, tertiary audiences with seasonality and spend implications. Use when family / domestic / international / HNWI segmentation matters.
- **Positioning — experience wheel / proposition matrix**: themes such as adventure, wellness, culture, nature, civic life, luxury. Use when the destination must balance multiple experiences without becoming incoherent.
- **Asset mix — district or village allocation matrix**: districts on rows, asset types on columns. Use to show how positioning translates into land use.
- **Phasing — asset-by-wave roadmap**: anchor assets, enabling infra, hospitality, public realm, activation calendar. Use when capex timing is a major lever.
- **Funding — development-play comparison matrix**: self-develop, land sale, land lease, JV, BOT. Use when the funding approach materially affects IRR and peak funding.
- **Socio-economic impact — waterfall or scorecard**: jobs, visitor spend, local SME impact, land value uplift, conservation value where relevant.
- **Governance — DevCo / DMO / operator interaction map**: use whenever the entity model is still contested.

## House conventions
- **Action-title format**: destination titles should marry identity and economics, e.g., “A phased nature-and-wellness destination anchored by three flagship experiences best balances differentiation and funding pressure.”
- **Executive summary structure**: role of destination, target visitor proposition, asset mix and phasing headline, funding logic, and near-term decisions.
- **How findings are articulated**: site / market fact → implication for positioning → implication for asset mix → implication for phasing and funding.
- **Tone**: specific, commercial, and place-aware. Avoid generic destination language that could apply anywhere.
- **Vocabulary**: “positioning,” “experience pillars,” “anchor assets,” “asset mix,” “development program,” “phasing,” “DevCo,” “DMO,” “operator strategy,” “peak funding.”
- **Storyline discipline**: positioning must precede asset mix; asset mix must precede financials.

## Inputs the skill needs
- **Site and context** (**Required**): location, land envelope, access, ownership, natural/cultural assets, constraints.
- **Strategic role** (**Required**): how the destination fits national, city, or investor objectives.
- **Demand and benchmark inputs** (**Required**): tourism flows, source markets, spending, benchmark destinations, surveys where available.
- **Early technical inputs** (**Optional but high value**): topography, geotechnical, environmental, infra, or land-use constraints.
- **Financial assumptions** (**Required**): capex ranges, operating assumptions, phasing, partnership options, funding constraints.
- **Institutional context** (**Optional but often material**): sponsor entity, proposed DevCo/DMO/operator model, board / committee structure.
- **Stakeholder sensitivities** (**Optional but high value**): community, regulator, environment, or heritage constraints.

## Methodology anchors
- Position the destination before debating asset mix; otherwise the program becomes a shopping list.
- Use benchmark destinations to infer success factors and phasing logic, not to copy assets one-for-one.
- Separate anchors from enabling real estate and infrastructure.
- Treat phasing as a financial lever, not just a construction sequence.
- Define the partner / operator / governance model early; destination viability often depends on operating quality as much as capex.
- Where the site is environmentally or culturally sensitive, design constraints belong in the main story, not just in technical appendix.
- Show why the destination matters in the broader tourism or urban system; standalone concepts are harder to defend.

## Done-state checklist
- The destination has a clear role and does not try to be every archetype at once.
- Target segments and their timing are explicit.
- Positioning clearly drives the asset mix.
- Asset phasing is realistic and linked to funding and absorption.
- Governance and operator roles are defined.
- Financials include alternative development plays and scenario logic.
- Environmental, heritage, or community constraints are explicit and reflected in design choices.
- The implementation path includes procurement / masterplanning / operator / investor actions.
- The summary answers both “Why will visitors come?” and “Why will the economics work?”
- The pack is still readable by a board that has not lived with the project for months.

## Composition notes
This skill commonly composes with **Strategic business case and feasibility study**, **Holding company / subsidiary governance and incorporation plan**, and **Final client readout / board decision deck**. Where the deliverable is really about a national tourism ecosystem rather than one destination, move up to **Sector development strategy and implementation playbook**.

## Metadata
- **Primary sectors:** tourism, real estate, public sector, culture and leisure  
- **Typical engagement duration:** 10–24 weeks depending on technical scope  
- **Typical team size:** 5–10 consultants plus technical / real estate / hospitality partners  
- **Primary consumer seniority:** manager / partner  
- **Decay risk:** medium  
- **Suggested owner discipline:** destination strategy and real estate
