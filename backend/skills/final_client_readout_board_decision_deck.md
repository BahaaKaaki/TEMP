# Final client readout / board decision deck

## When to use this skill
Use this at the end of a phase or engagement when the client needs a decisive synthesis for leadership, board, ExCom, or sponsor review. Typical trigger phrases include “final readout,” “leadership deck,” “board deck,” “decision meeting,” and “closing presentation.” Do **not** use it for weekly governance or for a still-open working draft; this deliverable exists to land the answer and secure approval or direction.

## Deliverable shape
1. **Executive recommendation and decisions required**  
   Purpose: state the answer and the asks immediately.  
   Typical length: 1–2 slides.  
   Complete when: the recommendation, decision points, and implications of inaction are explicit.  
   Common failure modes: summary of work done instead of answer given, no clear asks.

2. **Context refresher and case for change**  
   Purpose: remind leadership why this matters without re-teaching the full project.  
   Typical length: 1 slide.  
   Complete when: the strategic context and decision trigger are clear.  
   Common failure modes: too much backstory, no link to current decision.

3. **What was assessed and how confidence was built**  
   Purpose: establish credibility without drowning the audience in method.  
   Typical length: 1 slide.  
   Complete when: the main analyses, benchmarks, and stakeholder engagement are summarized with just enough proof.  
   Common failure modes: 5-slide methodology section, no confidence markers.

4. **Key findings / diagnosis**  
   Purpose: show the 3–5 findings that drive the answer.  
   Typical length: 2–4 slides.  
   Complete when: the findings are prioritized, evidence-backed, and directly linked to the recommendation.  
   Common failure modes: dumping all analyses, findings not sequenced, no “so what.”

5. **Options considered and evaluation logic**  
   Purpose: show that the recommended answer emerged from a fair comparison.  
   Typical length: 1–3 slides.  
   Complete when: viable options, evaluation criteria, and the comparative outcome are clear.  
   Common failure modes: no options shown, criteria hidden, recommendation looks predetermined.

6. **Recommended end-state / target model / strategic answer**  
   Purpose: articulate the chosen answer in concrete terms.  
   Typical length: 2–4 slides.  
   Complete when: the recommended strategy, model, or direction is described clearly enough to approve.  
   Common failure modes: answer still too high-level, no definition of target state.

7. **Impact, feasibility, and risk**  
   Purpose: show the practical consequence of choosing the answer.  
   Typical length: 1–3 slides.  
   Complete when: economics, service impact, capability implications, and major risks are visible.  
   Common failure modes: only upside, no risk, no feasibility lens.

8. **Roadmap and next steps**  
   Purpose: show how approval converts into action.  
   Typical length: 1–2 slides.  
   Complete when: immediate actions, milestones, and accountabilities are clear.  
   Common failure modes: “next steps” with no dates or owners.

9. **Appendix and backup**  
   Purpose: hold the proof points behind likely challenge questions.  
   Typical length: backup only.  
   Complete when: detailed benchmarks, calculations, sensitivity, and supporting analyses are available.  
   Common failure modes: no backup, or all proof left in the main story.

## Visual framework library
- **Page 1 — answer and ask**: recommendation, decision required, why now. This is the defining page.
- **Diagnosis — 3–5 headline finding pages**: each page should connect one fact pattern to one implication.
- **Option comparison — compact scorecard**: option, pros/cons, economics, risk, timing, recommendation.
- **Target-state articulation — strategy house / target-model page**: use the simplest format that makes the answer concrete.
- **Impact — headline bridge**: current state → recommendation impact → target state.
- **Roadmap — 90-day and 12-month view**: ideal when leadership wants the immediate next move.
- **Backup appendix — challenge-oriented**: benchmark details, scenario assumptions, deeper calculations, stakeholder evidence.

## House conventions
- **Action-title format**: the final deck must read like decisions, not analysis sections.
- **Executive summary structure**: answer, why it is the right answer, what leadership must approve.
- **How findings are articulated**: evidence → conclusion → implication for the decision.
- **Tone**: decisive, concise, and senior.
- **Vocabulary**: “recommendation,” “decision required,” “options considered,” “preferred path,” “implication,” “approval ask,” “implementation path.”
- **Board discipline**: anything not needed for approval moves to appendix.

## Inputs the skill needs
- **Final recommendation** (**Required**)
- **Priority findings and supporting exhibits** (**Required**)
- **Options / alternatives considered** (**Required**)
- **Impact / economics / risks** (**Required**)
- **Implementation steps and owners** (**Required**)
- **Backup appendix** (**Optional but strongly recommended**)

## Methodology anchors
- Start with the answer. Always.
- Reduce methodology to one credibility page.
- Show the rejected options; otherwise the recommendation feels untested.
- Keep the findings few and load-bearing.
- End with decisions and immediate next steps, not generic closing remarks.

## Done-state checklist
- The answer is visible on page 1.
- Leadership asks are explicit.
- The deck can survive “What options did you reject and why?”
- Key findings are prioritized and not buried.
- The target answer is concrete enough to approve.
- Risks and implementation burden are not hidden.
- Appendix support exists for expected challenge questions.

## Composition notes
