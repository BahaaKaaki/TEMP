# Holding company / subsidiary governance and incorporation plan

## When to use this skill

Use this when a parent company, sovereign-backed platform, giga-project, or holding company needs a repeatable governance model for multiple subsidiaries or needs to stand up a new holding and its operating doctrine. Typical trigger phrases include “subsidiary governance,” “holding company setup,” “group management doctrine,” “incorporation plan,” “DoA,” and “board / committee framework.” Do **not** use it for a single-entity operating model or a generic board-governance review.

## Deliverable shape

1. **Executive summary and governance doctrine recommendation**
  Purpose: state the recommended parent–subsidiary management model and why it fits the portfolio.  
   Typical length: 2–3 slides.  
   Complete when: the governance doctrine, expected level of parent involvement, and activation path are explicit.  
   Common failure modes: mixing up legal form and management doctrine, no rationale for the chosen model.
2. **Portfolio and subsidiary archetype mapping**
  Purpose: classify subsidiaries so governance is not one-size-fits-all.  
   Typical length: 2–3 slides.  
   Complete when: entities are segmented by ownership, maturity, business model, strategic importance, or risk profile.  
   Common failure modes: same governance for 100%-owned and minority stakes, no maturity lens, no archetypes.
3. **Group management doctrine and engagement model**
  Purpose: decide how hands-on the parent should be.  
   Typical length: 2–3 slides.  
   Complete when: the spectrum from financial holding to active oversight to operational involvement is visible, with the recommended doctrine by archetype.  
   Common failure modes: vague “strategic oversight,” no differentiation by subsidiary type, static doctrine across lifecycle.
4. **Governance bodies and decision-rights framework**
  Purpose: define where strategic, financial, risk, and operating decisions sit.  
   Typical length: 2–4 slides.  
   Complete when: board, committees, management, parent functions, and subsidiary leadership roles are clear, with escalation and reserved matters logic.  
   Common failure modes: only listing forums, no decision-right matrix, no minority-protection logic.
5. **Parent-to-subsidiary interaction model**
  Purpose: make the relationship operational.  
   Typical length: 2–3 slides.  
   Complete when: strategy, budget, talent, audit/risk, procurement, performance, and reporting interactions are defined.  
   Common failure modes: only legal reporting lines, no working model between corporate center and subsidiaries.
6. **Support-function model**
  Purpose: decide what is decentralized, in CoEs, or shared services.  
   Typical length: 1–3 slides.  
   Complete when: finance, HR, IT, legal, procurement, risk, and strategy roles are allocated logically with clear service model choices.  
   Common failure modes: over-centralization without capability, or excessive autonomy with no control.
7. **Legal structure and incorporation prerequisites**
  Purpose: specify what needs to be created or formalized.  
   Typical length: 2–3 slides.  
   Complete when: legal form options, ownership structure, capital, naming, required documents, and incorporation sequence are explicit.  
   Common failure modes: assuming legal teams will “figure it out,” no view on legal-form trade-offs, no incorporation readiness checklist.
8. **Organization, board secretariat, and control functions**
  Purpose: define the parent-company capabilities required to govern the portfolio.  
   Typical length: 1–3 slides.  
   Complete when: parent-company roles, board secretariat, subsidiary affairs, finance, risk, and governance support capabilities are identified.  
   Common failure modes: no corporate center design, no board-secretariat logic, governance assumed to sit in legal only.
9. **DoA toolkit and subsidiary playbook logic**
  Purpose: make the framework reusable across many entities.  
   Typical length: 2–3 slides.  
   Complete when: DoA variants, governance toolkits, and subsidiary-specific playbook generation logic are clear.  
   Common failure modes: one static DoA, no archetype-based variation, no reusable toolkit.
10. **Activation roadmap and transition**

Purpose: show how the parent and subsidiaries move to the new model.  
Typical length: 2–3 slides.  
Complete when: entity rollout, interim governance, documentation, training, and handover are phased.  
Common failure modes: target state only, no transition logic, no prioritization of which entities move first.

## Visual framework library

- **Archetype mapping — portfolio segmentation matrix**: subsidiaries by ownership / maturity / strategic relevance / risk. Use to prove governance should vary.
- **Doctrine — engagement-model continuum**: financial holding, strategic guidance, active oversight, operational involvement. Use when the parent is unclear on how hands-on to be.
- **Governance — reserved-matters matrix**: what stays at parent board, what sits with subsidiary board, what management can decide. Use whenever DoA discussions begin.
- **Interaction model — corporate-center heat map**: functions on rows, subsidiaries on columns, with type of interaction or control. Useful for budget, strategy, talent, and risk.
- **Support model — decentralized vs CoE vs shared-services comparison**: use when sponsor is debating service-center design.
- **Legal structure — decision tree**: LLC vs JSC vs listed / regulated form, minority protections, sector-specific constraints.
- **Toolkit — playbook builder**: archetype → governance bodies → DoA variant → reporting pack → cadence. Use when there are many entities to stand up.
- **Activation roadmap — wave rollout**: holding launch, first subsidiaries, next-wave entities, maturity shift from active oversight to strategic guidance.

## House conventions

- **Action-title format**: titles should capture the parent / subsidiary choice, e.g., “Active oversight at launch, shifting to strategic guidance by maturity, best balances control and speed.”
- **Executive summary structure**: doctrine, archetype logic, reserved matters, support model, and rollout sequence.
- **How findings are articulated**: portfolio fact → governance implication → doctrine / DoA / support-model consequence.
- **Tone**: legally aware but business-led; this is not just a compliance document.
- **Vocabulary**: “group management doctrine,” “reserved matters,” “active oversight,” “strategic guidance,” “subsidiary archetype,” “control model,” “board secretariat,” “DoA variant,” “incorporation readiness.”
- **Toolkit bias**: the deliverable should create a reusable operating logic, not a one-off memo.

## Inputs the skill needs

- **Portfolio view** (**Required**): current and planned subsidiaries, ownership model, maturity, strategic role, and sector.
- **Parent mandate** (**Required**): what the parent is expected to control, allocate, and challenge.
- **Current governance / legal baseline** (**Required**): where entities sit now, existing boards/committees/DoAs, and legal constraints.
- **Support-function baseline** (**Optional but high value**): how HR, finance, IT, legal, and strategy currently operate across the group.
- **Risk and stakeholder sensitivities** (**Required**): regulated sectors, minority shareholders, sovereign oversight, board expectations.
- **Rollout reality** (**Optional but valuable**): which entities need to be launched or restructured first, and by when.

## Methodology anchors

- Governance should vary by ownership, maturity, and risk profile; avoid a single doctrine for all entities.
- Active oversight is often appropriate at incorporation, but should not become permanent by default.
- Separate legal form decisions from management-doctrine decisions; they are related but not identical.
- Parent-company capability matters: subsidiary governance fails if no real center exists to run it.
- DoA should be modular, with variants by archetype and ownership, not one static matrix.
- Include minority-stake governance and shareholder protections when relevant; not every subsidiary is majority-owned.
- Treat incorporation prerequisites as work products, not assumptions.

## Done-state checklist

- The parent doctrine is explicit and differentiated by subsidiary archetype.
- Reserved matters and decision rights are clear.
- The interaction model covers strategy, budget, performance, talent, and risk, not just board reporting.
- The support-function model is practical.
- Legal and incorporation prerequisites are explicit.
- The parent-company capabilities needed to govern the portfolio are visible.
- A reusable toolkit / playbook logic exists.
- The rollout is phased and maturity-aware.
- The framework can answer “How will this work for a high-risk new subsidiary versus a mature operating one?”
- The output is usable by management, governance teams, and legal teams alike.

## Composition notes

This skill commonly composes with **Target operating model – design and activation blueprint** and **Board-ready way-forward / transaction option recommendation**. Use the TOM skill when the problem is one entity; use this skill when the real problem is parent–subsidiary architecture, governance, and rollout.
