# Organization Design – End-to-End

## When to use this skill

Use this skill when the client needs the full organization-design stack, not a single artifact: diagnose the current setup, benchmark peers, set design principles, define the target structure, detail roles, clarify governance and decision rights, size manpower, and translate the design into an activation plan. It is the right skill for ministry and authority transformations, entity setup, national champions, holding/subsidiary redesigns, shared-services restructurings, and post-strategy institutional redesign.

The stable end-to-end journey is: baseline → benchmark and gap analysis → design principles and options → target structure → detailing and functional statements → governance and DoA → manpower → activation. Do not use this skill when the ask is only one module, such as JD drafting, workload sizing, or committee design. Use the narrower sub-skills instead.

## Deliverable shape

### 1) Design brief and org questions

**Purpose**  
Anchor the org work in mandate, strategy, ecosystem role, maturity stage, and design questions.

**Typical length**  
1–2 slides.

**What must be present**  
What the entity must enable; the scope boundary; the business or institutional model; and the critical design questions such as centralization, control vs execution, subsidiary boundaries, field vs HQ split, or in-house vs outsourced.

**Common failure modes**  
Starting with an org chart before clarifying the entity’s role; using generic design language; or not stating the non-negotiables.

### 2) Baseline and pain-point diagnosis

**Purpose**  
Build a fact base on how the current organization actually works and where it breaks.

**Typical length**  
3–6 slides.

**What must be present**  
Current structure, role overlaps and gaps, spans and layers, governance and decision bottlenecks, current manpower distribution, current JDs or role definitions, key process or policy pain points, and stakeholder frustrations.

**Common failure modes**  
Treating the baseline as a document review only; missing informal power structures and workarounds; or jumping to solutions before isolating the real issues.

### 3) Benchmarking and gap analysis

**Purpose**  
Use external reference points to challenge the baseline and shape design choices.

**Typical length**  
2–5 slides.

**What must be present**  
Benchmark-selection logic; archetypes or peer set; extracted lessons on structure, governance, key positions, shared services, and decision rights; and an explicit current-vs-target gap synthesis.

**Common failure modes**  
Copying benchmark structures; using peers chosen only for familiarity; or failing to translate benchmark lessons into design implications.

### 4) Design principles and option set

**Purpose**  
Translate strategy and diagnosis into the criteria that should govern the design and the alternative structures worth considering.

**Typical length**  
2–4 slides.

**What must be present**  
Design imperatives; people-agnostic design principles; 2–4 credible structural options; and criteria for assessment such as strategic fit, accountability, agility, scalability, cost, spans of control, and disruption.

**Common failure modes**  
Using vague principles; offering one disguised option; or not making trade-offs explicit.

### 5) Target organization structure

**Purpose**  
Recommend the structural model and show how work groups into functions, departments, and reporting lines.

**Typical length**  
3–6 slides.

**What must be present**  
Selected structure; L1/L2 logic; where shared services sit; reporting lines; field vs HQ split where relevant; and a short-/medium-term evolution path if the target state will be phased.

**Common failure modes**  
Showing boxes without rationale; overdesigning levels too early; or leaving ambiguous boundaries between core and support functions.

### 6) Org detailing and functional statements

**Purpose**  
Turn the approved structure into something operable.

**Typical length**  
3–6 slides plus appendix.

**What must be present**  
Department- or role-level functional statements; accountabilities and interfaces; success measures or KPIs where appropriate; and delineation between adjacent units.

**Common failure modes**  
Functional statements that restate the title only; overlaps between neighboring units; or mixing department remits with person-specific tasks.

### 7) Governance, decision rights, and DoA

**Purpose**  
Clarify how decisions are made, escalated, and monitored across the new structure.

**Typical length**  
2–5 slides.

**What must be present**  
Boards/committees or equivalent forums; charters or terms of reference; authority matrix or management DoA; reporting cadence; and selected RACI logic for the most material cross-functional decisions.

**Common failure modes**  
Designing governance separately from structure; too many committees; or no clear distinction between approval, recommendation, and execution.

### 8) Job architecture and manpower

**Purpose**  
Translate the structure into positions, role logic, and staffing requirements over time.

**Typical length**  
3–6 slides plus model.

**What must be present**  
Job family or titling logic where in scope; role architecture or JDs for priority levels; manpower methodology; workload drivers or benchmark ratios; headcount by function and year; and budget implications.

**Common failure modes**  
Back-solving headcount to fit a number; inconsistent titling; or manpower plans that ignore the activity model and ramp-up sequence.

### 9) Migration and activation roadmap

**Purpose**  
Make the design executable.

**Typical length**  
2–4 slides.

**What must be present**  
Employee mapping logic; migration governance; quick wins; wave plan; decision gates; training or knowledge transfer needs; and a realistic activation roadmap.

**Common failure modes**  
Stopping at the target org; ignoring transition risks; or assuming staff can simply “move into boxes” without role, capability, and authority reset.

## Visual framework library

- **Org suite blueprint page** — one slide showing the full stack from baseline through activation, with icons or swimlanes by module. Use this on the first page of the workstream.
- **Current-state pain-point heat map** — functions on one axis and issue themes on the other (role clarity, span, decision rights, capability, manpower, processes); good for baseline synthesis.
- **Benchmark archetype matrix** — 2x2 or archetype cards showing structural logics such as functional, geography, customer, product, or hybrid. Use when the choice is about model type rather than direct peer copying.
- **Option-assessment scorecard** — rows for options and columns for design criteria, with short commentary on trade-offs. Use to avoid “management by preference.”
- **L1/L2/L3 org charts** — show increasing detail by page rather than one overloaded chart. Keep a legend for shared services, outsourced activities, and vacant/phase-2 roles.
- **Functional-statement cards** — department or role cards covering mandate, core accountabilities, key interfaces, and KPIs. Use in appendices or review packs.
- **Governance map** — board/committee hierarchy with escalation arrows and cadence labels; pair with a separate DoA or decision matrix.
- **Workload-to-FTE model** — waterfall from activity volumes to effort to FTEs to budget, or a by-function staffing ramp chart. Use when headcount needs to feel evidence-based.
- **Migration wave plan** — phased timeline showing mapping, selection, onboarding, policy/process reset, and training.

## House conventions

- Design the organization **people-agnostically**: roles first, incumbents second. This principle is explicit in multiple org-design cases and prevents political compromise from distorting the structure.
- Keep design principles visible throughout. In the corpus, strong teams explicitly use principles such as clarity of accountability, optimized spans of control, minimal layers, protection of critical specialists, and realism given culture and capabilities.
- Start from mandate and services, not from existing departments.
- Treat governance as part of org design, not as a later annex.
- Separate **structure** (who sits where), **functional statements** (what each unit is accountable for), **decision rights** (who can decide), and **manpower** (how many people are needed).
- Use phased end-states where the entity is immature or being set up from scratch; interim and steady-state structures often differ materially.
- When relevant, explicitly test what moves to shared services, what stays embedded, and what can be outsourced.

## Inputs the skill needs

- **Required**
  - Mandate, strategy, or target operating model context
  - Current org charts and role documentation if available
  - Stakeholder list for interviews
  - Scope boundary (holding only, subsidiaries too, HQ only, field included, etc.)
  - Timeline and maturity stage of the entity
- **Optional but high value**
  - Current headcount and cost data
  - Current JDs, competency models, grades, or titling rules
  - Current DoA, committee list, and process catalog
  - Prior benchmark studies
  - Transformation constraints (legal, labor, political, budgetary)

## Methodology anchors

This is the master skill for the suite. Invoke the narrower skills when the consultant only needs one sub-deliverable. The most common pairings are:

- with **Organization Baseline and Diagnostic** at the start,
- with **Target Organization Structure Design** and **Organization Detailing and Functional Statements** in the middle,
- with **Governance, Decision Rights, and Delegation of Authority** and **Manpower Planning and Sizing** before finalization,
- and with **Staff Migration and Org Activation** to close the loop.

## Done-state checklist

- Primary sectors: cross-sector  
- Typical engagement duration: 6–20 weeks for design; longer if activation support is included  
- Typical team size: 3–8 core team members plus SMEs  
- Primary consumer seniority: associate / manager / principal  
- Decay risk: low  
- Suggested owner discipline: organization design / operating model / people & organization

## Design principles

- Use a **baseline → benchmark → design → activate** logic; do not start with end-state structure design.
- Always separate mandate questions from structure questions. Clarify role in the ecosystem or value chain before deciding where functions sit.
- Make centralization/shared-services choices explicit, not implicit. Test what should be centralized, retained, or outsourced.
- Use design criteria to compare options, not preference-based debate. Common criteria include strategic fit, accountability, efficiency, agility, disruption, span of control, and scalability.
- Detail the structure only after top-level choices are agreed; a stable pattern is L1/L2 first, then N-2/N-3, then functional statements and JDs.
- Manpower should be workload- or driver-based where possible, with benchmark ratios as a check rather than the only method.
- Activation is not a footer. Migration, mapping, training, and governance reset are part of the org deliverable.

## Composition notes

- The design brief is anchored in mandate, services, and strategic priorities.
- The baseline clearly identifies role, span, governance, and manpower pain points.
- Benchmarking is relevant and translated into design implications, not copied.
- The design principles are explicit and visible in the option assessment.
- The chosen structure has clear logic at L1/L2 and a credible path to lower levels.
- Functional statements eliminate overlaps and blank spaces between adjacent units.
- Decision rights, committees, and escalation logic are defined for the material decisions.
- Headcount is evidence-based and tied to the activity model and phase of maturity.
- The activation plan makes the design implementable within the client’s constraints.
