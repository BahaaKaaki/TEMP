# Proposal Development

## When to use this skill
Use this skill to build a full Strategy& proposal pack: technical proposals, RFP responses, scope expansions, unsolicited credentials-led proposals, and senior discussion packs that need to reassure the client on both thinking and delivery. It is the right skill when the document must do three things at once: show that we understand the problem, show that we have a sharp point of view on what matters, and show that we can deliver a disciplined workplan. The most stable proposal spine is: executive summary → understanding of the situation → perspective → Why Strategy& → approach → team and governance.

Use it when the client needs confidence in the full story. Do not use it for a pure credentials deck, a pure POV paper, or an isolated workplan page. Those should invoke narrower skills such as Proof-Led Relevance, Executive Top-Down, or Phase-Spine Implementation.

## Proposal shape

### 1) Recommendation / stance
**Purpose**  
Open with what the client should do, why now, and the logic for why our approach fits.

**Typical length**  
1 slide or 0.5–1 page.

**What must be present**  
A clear answer, not a process statement; the client objective reframed into a decision; and 3–5 reasons the answer is credible.

**Common failure modes**  
Leading with “we propose to support” instead of the answer; sounding like a boilerplate submission; or burying the recommendation until later.

### 2) 0) Executive summary
**Purpose**  
Act as the verbal table of contents for the entire pack.

**Typical length**  
1–2 slides.

**What must be present**  
The section is numbered `0)`; each bullet mirrors the exact phrasing of the sections that follow; and each bullet previews what will be decided or delivered, not just what will be discussed.

**Common failure modes**  
Summarizing content rather than landing a decision; using generic labels (“Our approach”, “Our team”); or repeating the brief without showing a point of view.

### 3) Our understanding of the situation
**Purpose**  
Show that we understand the client context, why the issue matters, what constraints shape the answer, and where the real pressure points sit.

**Typical length**  
3–8 slides.

**What must be present**  
Evidence-backed situation framing; client-specific dynamics; implications of those dynamics; and a clear bridge to why the proposed work is needed.

**Common failure modes**  
Turning the section into sector trivia; overloading it with external facts that do not change the recommendation; or stopping at diagnosis without surfacing what the diagnosis implies.

### 4) Our perspective
**Purpose**  
Translate context into choices, design principles, and strategic logic.

**Typical length**  
3–8 slides.

**What must be present**  
A crisp POV on what matters most; the logic for how the problem should be tackled; and the criteria or principles that will govern the work.

**Common failure modes**  
Repeating the “understanding” section; offering generic best practice rather than a tailored perspective; or showing themes without the forced choices beneath them.

### 5) Why Strategy&
**Purpose**  
Prove that we are the right team for this problem, this client, and this delivery risk profile.

**Typical length**  
3–10 slides in the main pack, with more in the appendix if needed.

**What must be present**  
3–5 proof-led pillars; each pillar tied to a client need or delivery risk; selected credentials, tools, ecosystem familiarity, or execution capability that substantiate the point.

**Common failure modes**  
Generic firm claims; too many logos without relevance; credentials that are close in sector but not in problem shape; or no explicit connection to delivery risk.

### 6) Proposed approach
**Purpose**  
Show how the work will be done from mobilization through final outputs.

**Typical length**  
3–8 slides.

**What must be present**  
A 3–5 phase backbone; for each phase, linked activities, deliverables, decision points, timing, and stakeholder engagement moments.

**Common failure modes**  
Workstreams and timeline that do not map to the same phases; pretty chevrons with no decision logic; or outputs that are too abstract to feel real.

### 7) Team structure and governance
**Purpose**  
Show how the work will be run and how quality, escalation, and decisions will be handled.

**Typical length**  
2–4 slides.

**What must be present**  
Forums and cadence; decision rights and escalation; core team coverage; QA flow; and the client-side role in making the engagement work.

**Common failure modes**  
An org chart with no operating model; governance that feels bolted on; or team pages that are all biographies and no delivery logic.

### 8) Appendix / selected qualifications
**Purpose**  
Provide enough proof and detail to de-risk the client decision without bloating the main story.

**Typical length**  
As needed.

**What must be present**  
Only the supporting material that reinforces the main pitch: closest qualifications, deeper methodology, and selected toolkits.

**Common failure modes**  
Dumping every credential available; introducing new messages in the appendix; or relying on appendix depth to compensate for a weak main story.

## Page patterns and exhibit types
Use a small set of recurring page types.

- **Answer-first summary page** — one message headline, 3–5 supporting bullets, and selective proof points.
- **Situation / implication page** — outside-in facts on the left, implications or forced decisions on the right.
- **Proposal chevron** — 3–5 phases; each phase annotated with activities, outputs, and milestones.
- **RFP mapping matrix** — requirement-by-requirement view if the client is compliance-sensitive.
- **Why Strategy& proof page** — one claim headline, 2–4 proof bullets, and 1–2 most relevant credentials.
- **Governance page** — forums, cadence, decision rights, and escalation flow, not just boxes and lines.
- **Timeline page** — integrated with the phases, workshops, steerco points, and output drops.

Prefer one idea per page. If a page is doing more than one job, it is usually under-edited.

## House conventions
- Start with the answer, not with the team or the process.
- Keep the Executive summary inside the main narrative and number it `0)`.
- Use action-title headlines that are declarative, specific, and ideally decision-relevant.
- Keep 3–5 main pillars. More than that usually dilutes the story.
- Make phases the single backbone: activities, deliverables, timeline, governance, and resourcing all map to them.
- Use named assumptions only where they change scope, cost, timing, or risk.
- Keep “Why Strategy&” proof-led and risk-linked; do not use generic adjectives without evidence.

## Inputs the skill needs
**Required**
- Client context: sector, geography, ownership, maturity, and why the issue matters now.
- Scope and deliverables: what is in, what is out, and what decision the proposal must support.
- Timeline and constraints: deadlines, governance requirements, data access, and client-side dependencies.
- Known hypotheses or partner point of view.
- Relevant internal proof points.

**Optional**
- Detailed RFP text.
- Pricing and commercials.
- Draft staffing model.
- Prior relationship context.
- Competitor signals.

The skill should degrade gracefully if commercials are absent; it should not degrade gracefully if the decision to be made is unclear.

## Judgment anchors
- Calibrate the proposal to the moment: pursuit, shortlist, final technical response, or board reassurance pack.
- Decide whether the client first needs reassurance on our understanding, our delivery discipline, or our relevance. The proposal should lean accordingly.
- Pick 3–5 phases only; more than that tends to signal activity rather than control.
- If the RFP is ambiguous, restate objectives, deliverables, timeline, governance, and dependencies before drafting the full story.
- Use only those credentials that reduce a visible risk for the client.

## Common failure modes
- The recommendation appears too late.
- The Executive summary reads like admin, not a decision narrative.
- “Our understanding” is generic market context with no client consequence.
- “Our perspective” repeats the brief instead of offering a point of view.
- “Why Strategy&” is a logo page.
- The approach lacks a single backbone.
- Governance and quality assurance are thin.
- The appendix carries the real story because the front end is underpowered.

## Done-state checklist
- The first page answers the client’s real question.
- The Executive summary mirrors the exact storyline that follows.
- Every major section has one job and does it cleanly.
- The approach can be read in 30 seconds and still make operational sense.
- The Why Strategy& section would still be convincing if all logos were removed and only the proof remained.
- The team and governance pages make the engagement feel controlled.
- The proposal reads as one story, not as stitched-together modules.
- A skeptical partner could challenge any key claim and find proof or a named assumption.

## Composition notes
This is the parent skill. It should almost always compose with:
- **Executive Top-Down / Answer-First Narrative**
- **Situation–Implication–Response Narrative**
- **Phase-Spine Implementation Narrative**
- **Proof-Led Relevance Narrative (“Why Strategy&”)**

## Metadata
- Primary use: proposals and RFP responses
- Typical consumer seniority: manager to partner
- Typical output length: 10–40 slides
- Durability: high
- Suggested owner discipline: commercial excellence / proposal excellence
