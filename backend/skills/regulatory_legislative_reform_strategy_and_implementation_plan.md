# Regulatory / legislative reform strategy and implementation plan

## When to use this skill
Use this when a government, regulator, royal-court function, or policy owner needs to redesign the rule-making architecture, licensing regime, or broader legislative “machine.” Typical trigger phrases include “legislative reform,” “licensing framework,” “regulatory reform,” “rulebook architecture,” and “reform program.” Do **not** use it for a standard sector strategy where regulation is only one enabling pillar, or for a narrow policy paper with no implementation mandate.

## Deliverable shape
1. **Executive summary and reform case**  
   Purpose: state why reform is needed, what will change, and how reform will be governed.  
   Typical length: 2–3 slides.  
   Complete when: the summary names the reform logic, priority components, and the implementation path.  
   Common failure modes: legalese, no case for change, no sequencing.

2. **Baseline of current framework / process / institutions**  
   Purpose: show how the current system performs today.  
   Typical length: 3–5 slides.  
   Complete when: the rulebook / licensing framework, process steps, institutional mandates, and pain points are mapped.  
   Common failure modes: current-state description without diagnosis, no end-to-end process view, no distinction between law, regulation, policy, and guidance.

3. **Benchmarking and global reform lessons**  
   Purpose: learn from comparable jurisdictions and reform programs.  
   Typical length: 2–4 slides.  
   Complete when: benchmarks are selected for relevance, not prestige, and lessons are translated into design implications.  
   Common failure modes: copying a jurisdiction wholesale, no local transfer logic, no reform-program benchmark.

4. **Gap analysis and prioritization of issues**  
   Purpose: make the reform problem concrete.  
   Typical length: 2–3 slides.  
   Complete when: gaps are assessed across instruments, process, institutions, capabilities, and performance.  
   Common failure modes: gap list with no severity or priority, no evidence base.

5. **Target-state design for rules / instruments / process**  
   Purpose: define how the regulatory or legislative system should work.  
   Typical length: 3–5 slides.  
   Complete when: instrument taxonomy, hierarchy, process stages, quality requirements, consultation logic, and monitoring are clear.  
   Common failure modes: target state too abstract, process not operational, taxonomy unresolved.

6. **Institutional setup and governance model**  
   Purpose: define which entities do what and how the reform is steered.  
   Typical length: 2–4 slides.  
   Complete when: mandates, roles, governance bodies, interactions, and capability needs are explicit.  
   Common failure modes: no owner for reform, too many bodies, unclear parent / regulator / delivery-unit split.

7. **Prioritization framework and reform portfolio**  
   Purpose: convert the target state into a phased reform agenda.  
   Typical length: 2–3 slides.  
   Complete when: reforms or licensing modules are prioritized by impact, urgency, and feasibility.  
   Common failure modes: all reforms treated equally, no staging, no decision logic.

8. **Capability building, change management, and socialization**  
   Purpose: make the reform adoptable.  
   Typical length: 1–3 slides.  
   Complete when: training, guidance, playbooks, stakeholder engagement, and change actions are defined.  
   Common failure modes: assuming legal reform self-implements, no capability-building plan, no committee/socialization path.

9. **KPI, risk, and assurance framework**  
   Purpose: track reform performance and manage delivery risk.  
   Typical length: 1–2 slides.  
   Complete when: service / timing / quality / adoption KPIs, risk registers, and review cadence are specified.  
   Common failure modes: no metrics beyond milestone tracking, no risk ownership.

10. **Implementation roadmap and first-wave actions**  
Purpose: move reform from paper to execution.  
Typical length: 2–3 slides.  
Complete when: waves, quick wins, priority entities, drafting work, consultations, and operating-model setup are sequenced.  
Common failure modes: no first 6–12 month actions, no transition from design to institutionalization.

## Visual framework library
- **Baseline — legislative machine map**: issue identification → policy formulation → drafting → review → approval / issuance → implementation / monitoring. Use to show end-to-end friction.
- **Framework — instrument taxonomy / hierarchy chart**: law, regulation, rule, circular, guideline, etc. Use when the stack of instruments is confused or overlapping.
- **Gap analysis — eight-foundation heat map**: predictability, quality, consultation, timeliness, impact realization, capability, etc. Use when the reform needs a structured diagnostic.
- **Institutional design — governance ecosystem map**: entities by role (policy, drafting, review, approval, implementation, monitoring).
- **Prioritization — impact × urgency × feasibility matrix**: useful when multiple reform tracks or licensing modules compete for early attention.
- **Roadmap — reform waves**: foundational taxonomy / process cleanup, institutional setup, capability building, digitization, continuous improvement.
- **KPI dashboard — service / quality / adoption view**: processing time, compliance, consultation quality, publication / update discipline, training / awareness, issue closure. Use where implementation office maturity is low.

## House conventions
- **Action-title format**: titles should describe how the machine should change, e.g., “A standardized hierarchy of instruments and a single end-to-end process are prerequisites for legislative quality and speed.”
- **Executive summary structure**: case for reform, target-state logic, institutional setup, reform waves, and first decisions.
- **How findings are articulated**: baseline weakness → institutional or process implication → target-state design choice.
- **Tone**: precise, low-drama, and implementation-ready; avoid legal drafting language unless the point is specifically jurisprudential.
- **Vocabulary**: “legislative machine,” “rule-making instruments,” “taxonomy,” “hierarchy,” “institutional setup,” “consultation,” “institutionalization guidelines,” “wholesale reform program,” “reserved matters,” “quality assurance.”
- **Change bias**: legal reform decks should show how adoption will happen, not just what the final architecture should be.

## Inputs the skill needs
- **Scope of reform** (**Required**): licensing regime, legislative process, instrument stack, or broader reform program.
- **Current-state documentation** (**Required**): laws, regulations, manuals, process notes, licensing criteria, org charts, or committee structures.
- **Stakeholder map** (**Required**): policymakers, regulators, drafting bodies, approvers, implementers, and affected market participants.
- **Benchmark jurisdictions** (**Optional but high value**): candidate comparators or relevant reform programs.
- **Political / legal constraints** (**Required**): approval pathways, non-negotiables, constitutional or sector limits.
- **Reform urgency and timeline** (**Optional**): whether the sponsor needs a full program or a first-wave fix.
- **Capability baseline** (**Optional but useful**): current staffing, legal drafting capability, regulatory analytics, and PMO capacity.

## Methodology anchors
- Reform the full machine: instruments, process, institutions, capability, and program governance must be treated separately.
- Benchmark reform programs and institutional arrangements, not just the text of laws.
- Keep taxonomy and hierarchy explicit; many reform programs fail because the instrument stack remains muddled.
- Prioritize reforms visibly; all legal problems are not equal.
- Build in consultation, institutionalization, and capability transfer, especially where multiple entities co-own the process.
- Make implementation concrete with a delivery office, wave plan, and KPI logic.

## Done-state checklist
- The reform case is explicit and grounded in current-state pain points.
- The instrument hierarchy and process design are clear.
- Institutional roles are unambiguous.
- Prioritization logic is visible.
- The change / socialization plan is practical.
- KPIs track quality and adoption, not just milestones.
- The roadmap names the first-wave reforms and owners.
- The deck can answer “How will this change day-to-day rule making or licensing behavior?”
- The sponsor’s role versus the delivery office’s role is explicit.
- The package is detailed enough to survive legal and executive scrutiny.

## Composition notes
This skill commonly composes with **Sector development strategy and implementation playbook**, **Kick-off, workplan and data request pack**, and **Final client readout / board decision deck**. Use the sector-playbook skill if regulation is only one section of the strategy; use this one if regulation and institutional reform are the central deliverable.

- **Primary consumer seniority:** manager / partner
