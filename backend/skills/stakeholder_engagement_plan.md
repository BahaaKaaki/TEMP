# Stakeholder Engagement Plan

## When to use this skill
Use this skill when the client needs a structured way to identify, prioritize, and manage a complex stakeholder ecosystem across a strategy, investment, activation, transformation, or development agenda. It is especially useful when the work spans multiple public entities, regulators, operators, funders, and delivery partners and the client needs a practical answer to three questions: who matters, why they matter, and how engagement should evolve over time.

This is a distinct deliverable, not just a PMO sub-task. The canonical shape is a four-step framework from strategic direction to interaction timeline, translated into a concrete engagement plan that evolves across planning, construction, and operations phases. Use this skill when the output must stand on its own as a management tool. Do not use it for adoption-heavy change programs, investor outreach, or policy consultation models; those should invoke the related skills built for those shapes.

## Deliverable shape

### 1) Objective, scope, and strategic context
**Purpose**  
Anchor the stakeholder plan in the client’s strategic objective, project scope, and lifecycle.

**Typical length**  
1 slide or 0.5–1 page.

**What must be present**  
The initiative context; why stakeholder orchestration matters to success; and the lifecycle or workstreams the plan must support.

**Common failure modes**  
Jumping straight into a stakeholder list with no strategic logic; confusing “stakeholder management” with generic communications; or failing to define the phases the plan must cover.

### 2) Stakeholder universe and long-list
**Purpose**  
Create the full stakeholder universe before prioritizing.

**Typical length**  
1–3 slides.

**What must be present**  
A comprehensive long-list grouped by ecosystem role, such as governing entities, planning and development entities, operators and service providers, funders, regulators, or delivery partners.

**Common failure modes**  
Listing only the obvious ministries or executives; omitting enabling entities such as utilities, permitting bodies, financiers, or local operators; or failing to distinguish institutions from named representatives.

### 3) Role and mandate mapping
**Purpose**  
Clarify what each stakeholder actually does in relation to the initiative.

**Typical length**  
2–5 slides.

**What must be present**  
A concise statement of role, mandate, approval power, dependency, or contribution at the relevant stage of the lifecycle.

**Common failure modes**  
Relying on labels rather than mandates; treating all stakeholders as equal; or not linking mandates to concrete project moments such as feasibility review, licensing, financing, operations, or public service provision.

### 4) Categorization and prioritization
**Purpose**  
Move from the long-list to a management view of who matters most and how.

**Typical length**  
1–3 slides.

**What must be present**  
A clear logic for categorization, usually a 2x2 or equivalent based on influence/impact and involvement/interest or criticality; stakeholder types such as contributing, enabling, and supporting; and an explicit rationale for where each group sits.

**Common failure modes**  
Using a generic influence–interest matrix without tailoring it to the client’s lifecycle; mixing up institutional power with day-to-day involvement; or creating categories that do not change the engagement model.

### 5) Engagement objectives and mechanisms by stakeholder type
**Purpose**  
Define what engagement is meant to achieve for each category and how that engagement will happen.

**Typical length**  
2–4 slides.

**What must be present**  
For each stakeholder type: engagement objective, typical mechanisms, decision topics, forum type, and indicative communication frequency.

**Common failure modes**  
Assuming “high-frequency engagement” is always better; using one channel mix for all stakeholders; or not distinguishing between informing, aligning, co-designing, approving, and resolving blockers.

### 6) Phase-based engagement timeline
**Purpose**  
Show how engagement evolves across the program lifecycle.

**Typical length**  
1–3 slides.

**What must be present**  
A time-based or phase-based timeline covering the full lifecycle, with stakeholder categories mapped against phases such as planning, funding, development, construction, launch, operations, or activation.

**Common failure modes**  
Treating engagement as static; showing only a first-phase plan; or failing to reflect that some stakeholders matter intensely only at specific moments.

### 7) Governance, tracking, and review model
**Purpose**  
Ensure stakeholder management is monitored, not just designed.

**Typical length**  
1–2 slides.

**What must be present**  
KPI owners, review cadence, data sources, escalation routes, and feedback loops to update the plan as the program evolves.

**Common failure modes**  
No owner for the plan; no reporting rhythm; or a plan that is too qualitative to be managed.

### 8) Activation plan, resources, and knowledge transfer
**Purpose**  
Turn the framework into a live operating tool.

**Typical length**  
1–2 slides.

**What must be present**  
Immediate next steps, resource implications, templates or trackers to be used, and a handover or capability-building path.

**Common failure modes**  
Delivering a static strategy artifact without activation mechanics; no templates; or no capability transfer to the client team.

## Page patterns and exhibit types
Use a small set of recurring exhibit types.

- **Stakeholder ecosystem map** — grouped by role in the value chain or program lifecycle.
- **Role and mandate table** — stakeholder, mandate, dependency, approval point, and implication for the project.
- **Prioritization 2x2** — usually influence/impact vs involvement/interest or criticality.
- **Category logic page** — contributing / enabling / supporting (or equivalent) with definitions.
- **Communication objective and frequency matrix** — objective, forum type, and high / moderate / low cadence.
- **Phase-based engagement heatmap or timeline** — stakeholder categories across planning, construction, operations or equivalent phases.
- **Tracking dashboard concept** — KPI owners, reporting cadence, and corrective-action loop.

Prefer lifecycle-linked visuals over abstract stakeholder theory.

## House conventions
- Start with the client objective, not with the stakeholder list.
- Map stakeholders to the actual lifecycle of the initiative: planning, funding, enabling, development, operations, or analogous phases.
- Separate role mapping from prioritization. First understand mandates, then classify.
- Use a small number of stakeholder types that clearly change the engagement model.
- Define communication objectives explicitly: inform, align, co-create, approve, unblock, or monitor.
- Frequency must be purposeful. “High” should mean decision-heavy or risk-heavy, not simply senior.
- Build a live update mechanism; the plan should evolve with the program.
- Keep the plan institutional, not person-dependent, unless the assignment explicitly requires named relationship owners.

## Inputs the skill needs
**Required**
- Client mandate and project objective.
- Scope and lifecycle phases the initiative will go through.
- Initial stakeholder universe or at least known stakeholder categories.
- Client view of approvals, dependencies, and bottlenecks.
- Any existing engagement mechanisms or governance forums already in place.

**Optional**
- Existing org charts and government mapping.
- Current communication cadences and meeting calendars.
- Relationship history with key stakeholders.
- Budget and resourcing constraints.
- KPI or reporting requirements.

The skill should degrade gracefully if the initial stakeholder list is incomplete; it should not degrade gracefully if the initiative phases are unclear.

## Judgment anchors
- Decide whether the right first cut is by institutional role, value-chain role, lifecycle role, or geography.
- Distinguish carefully between powerful stakeholders with episodic involvement and operational stakeholders with continuous involvement.
- Make the categories useful. If a category does not change engagement objective, channel, or frequency, it is too abstract.
- Use the plan to expose coordination risk, not just communications effort.
- Keep the plan manageable: prioritize the few relationships that can block the program.
- Design the cadence to fit the client’s real decision cycle, not an idealized consulting calendar.

## Common failure modes
- The plan is just a contact list.
- Stakeholders are categorized without understanding their mandate.
- Frequency is defined with no rationale.
- The timeline shows engagement but not why each moment matters.
- The plan has no review mechanism and goes stale quickly.
- The client cannot tell who owns the relationship, who owns the tracking, and what success looks like.

## Done-state checklist
- The stakeholder universe feels complete enough to manage real risk.
- Each priority stakeholder has a clear role and implication for the program.
- Categorization changes the engagement model in a visible way.
- Engagement objectives are explicit and phase-specific.
- The timeline reflects how stakeholder needs change over time.
- Tracking and quarterly review mechanics are defined.
- The artifact can be used by a client team tomorrow without rethinking the logic.
- A skeptical sponsor can see where misalignment risk sits and how it will be managed.

## Composition notes
This skill should often compose with:
- **Stakeholder Workshop and Socialization Plan**
- **Governance Cadence and Decision Forum Design**
- **Change Management and Communication Plan**

It is the parent skill for internal and public-ecosystem engagement. Use **Investor and Partner Engagement Strategy** instead when the core objective is capital attraction, operator selection, or investor onboarding.

## Metadata
- Primary use: multi-stakeholder strategies, public-sector programs, destination development, transformation programs
- Typical consumer seniority: associate to director
- Typical output length: 6–15 slides
- Durability: high
- Suggested owner discipline: PMO / strategy execution / public-sector transformation
