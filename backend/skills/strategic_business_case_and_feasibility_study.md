# Strategic business case and feasibility study

## When to use this skill
Use this when the core question is whether a concept, asset, new entity, service line, or investment move is viable and worth approving. Typical trigger phrases include “feasibility study,” “business case,” “go/no-go,” “green light,” “launch case,” and “investment approval.” Do **not** use it for a full enterprise strategy refresh where the client already exists and the question is directional choice rather than concept viability; use the corporate strategy skill instead.

## Deliverable shape
1. **Executive recommendation and approval ask**  
   Purpose: tell the decision-maker whether to proceed, under what conditions, and with which model.  
   Typical length: 2–3 slides.  
   Complete when: the headline recommendation, gating assumptions, economics range, and next approval decisions are explicit.  
   Common failure modes: summary of analysis without a recommendation, or recommendation without caveats.

2. **Decision framing and concept scope**  
   Purpose: define what exactly is being tested and why now.  
   Typical length: 1–2 slides.  
   Complete when: the concept boundary, decision questions, alternatives in scope, and approval objective are clear.  
   Common failure modes: unclear scope, hidden assumptions, confusion between business case and technical design study.

3. **Demand and market attractiveness**  
   Purpose: prove there is a real demand pool and explain how much is addressable.  
   Typical length: 2–4 slides.  
   Complete when: demand is sized, segmented, timed, and linked to actual adoption or absorption logic.  
   Common failure modes: top-down market size only, no willingness-to-pay logic, no timing assumptions.

4. **Supply, competition, and benchmark landscape**  
   Purpose: understand what the concept competes against and what success looks like elsewhere.  
   Typical length: 2–4 slides.  
   Complete when: current supply, benchmark analogues, whitespace, and competitive response risk are visible.  
   Common failure modes: no benchmark analogues, generic competitor list, weak differentiation logic.

5. **Concept, value proposition, and business model options**  
   Purpose: translate the opportunity into an actual proposition and operating logic.  
   Typical length: 2–4 slides.  
   Complete when: the concept is specific, customer segments are defined, and structural options (standalone, JV, outsource, managed service, etc.) are compared.  
   Common failure modes: concept still reads like a slogan, business model is implied not stated, no option comparison.

6. **Operating model and delivery requirements**  
   Purpose: show what it would take to make the concept work in practice.  
   Typical length: 1–3 slides.  
   Complete when: the core capabilities, staffing model, partner roles, governance, and material process requirements are identified.  
   Common failure modes: overly detailed org chart too early, or no delivery reality at all.

7. **Technical / asset / service requirements**  
   Purpose: capture the infrastructure, product, asset, or technical requirements that drive feasibility.  
   Typical length: 1–3 slides.  
   Complete when: major design assumptions, capacity requirements, or critical technology dependencies are explicit.  
   Common failure modes: technical design taking over the whole story, or no technical grounding for cost assumptions.

8. **Financial model, scenarios, and sensitivities**  
   Purpose: show whether the concept works financially and what drives the answer.  
   Typical length: 3–5 slides.  
   Complete when: revenues, cost structure, capex, working capital where relevant, NPV/IRR/payback, and base/upside/downside scenarios are visible.  
   Common failure modes: only one scenario, no sensitivity view, no separation of run-rate versus one-time items.

9. **Risk assessment and mitigations**  
   Purpose: show what could break the case and what reduces that risk.  
   Typical length: 1–2 slides.  
   Complete when: the top commercial, operational, regulatory, and execution risks are tied to mitigations or gating actions.  
   Common failure modes: generic risk boilerplate, no commercial gating assumptions, no decision implications.

10. **Recommended option and implementation roadmap**  
Purpose: convert a positive case into an executable path.  
Typical length: 2–4 slides.  
Complete when: the deck shows the recommended model, first-wave actions, owners, and major milestones from approval through launch.  
Common failure modes: the business case ends at “yes,” no launch logic, no sequence of enabling actions.

## Visual framework library
- **Executive summary — proceed / do not proceed page**: headline recommendation with conditions, economics range, and top 3 risks. Use when the decision is binary or there is one recommended model.
- **Demand — market sizing waterfall**: total market → eligible market → addressable market → achievable volume. Use instead of a single TAM bubble.
- **Competition — whitespace map**: 2x2 or coverage map showing current offerings by segment/theme/price point. Use when the point is absence or fragmentation of current supply.
- **Business model options — archetype comparison matrix**: compare standalone, JV, outsourced, BOT, franchise, shared services, or managed service models across control, economics, capability needs, and speed. Use whenever structural choices are in play.
- **Technical requirements — capacity ladder / asset mix table**: convert demand assumptions into rooms, beds, outlets, keys, seats, classrooms, storage, or platform capacity. Use when volume must be translated into operating assets.
- **Financials — scenario table plus tornado sensitivity**: show base, upside, downside and 5–7 critical levers. Use this instead of hiding all uncertainty in footnotes.
- **Risk page — gating assumption map**: assumptions grouped into “must prove before approval,” “must lock before launch,” and “manage during operation.” Use when the audience is investment committee or board.
- **Roadmap — approval-to-launch roadmap**: design, procurement, licensing, staffing, systems, go-live. Use when the implementation burden is a major part of the answer.

## House conventions
- **Action-title format**: titles should read like an investment memo, e.g., “The concept is viable only if the client adopts an operator-led model and phases capex over two waves.”
- **Executive summary structure**: 1 slide for answer, 1 slide for economics/risk, 1 slide for key actions if the audience is senior enough.
- **How findings are articulated**: each analytical page should end in a feasibility implication, not just a number.
- **Tone**: commercially hard-headed. Feasibility packs should not overstate upside or hide the downside case.
- **Vocabulary**: “concept,” “approval ask,” “business model,” “gating assumptions,” “sensitivity,” “pilot / scale-up,” “operator model,” “funding need,” “implementation burden.”
- **Evidence discipline**: every major revenue or cost line should be traceable to a demand, benchmark, technical, or capability assumption.

## Inputs the skill needs
- **Client context** (**Required**): sector, geography, target concept or asset, sponsor, and the approval objective.
- **Decision question** (**Required**): green light, concept comparison, business model choice, scale decision, or phasing decision.
- **Market data** (**Required**): demand, competitive landscape, pricing benchmarks, adoption patterns, or at least credible proxies.
- **Operating assumptions** (**Required**): capacity, staffing, utilization, service scope, operating hours, or technical design assumptions.
- **Financial assumptions** (**Required**): capex, opex, revenue drivers, and funding assumptions.
- **Existing studies** (**Optional but high value**): previous market studies, DD material, concept notes, technical design, or board packs.
- **Leadership hypotheses** (**Optional**): strong prior views on model or risk appetite.
- **Constraints** (**Required**): regulatory, capex ceiling, speed-to-launch, land/site constraints, or ownership rules.

## Methodology anchors
- Demand needs timing, not just size: show when volumes arrive and what adoption curve makes them credible.
- Translate concept into assets or operating capacity early; otherwise financials float free of reality.
- Compare structural options explicitly; do not hide the real decision inside the recommendation.
- Separate base case from “aspirational” case; boards are more persuaded by downside realism than by aggressive upside.
- Sensitivity analysis should focus on the 5–7 variables that actually move the answer, not every spreadsheet input.
- Convert risks into actions and gates: what must be proven before approval, before procurement, and before launch.
- If the concept is only viable with material policy support, anchor that visibly rather than burying it in assumptions.

## Done-state checklist
- The pack clearly answers “Should we do this?” and “Under what model?”
- Demand, competition, technical assumptions, and financials are logically linked.
- Structural options were evaluated fairly before the recommendation was made.
- The economics are shown under at least three scenarios.
- The biggest risks are visible and have practical mitigations or gating actions.
- The recommendation includes a launch path, not just a business case.
- The summary can stand in front of an investment committee or board without re-explaining the methodology.
- The case does not rely on a single heroic assumption.
- Titles land implications, not just analysis steps.
- The appendix contains detail, but the main pack is decision-ready.

## Composition notes
This skill commonly composes with **Target operating model – design and activation blueprint**, **Final client readout / board decision deck**, and **Board-ready way-forward / transaction option recommendation**. Use the latter when the business case is really a constrained choice among contractual or transaction pathways rather than a greenfield concept.

## Metadata
- **Primary sectors:** cross-sector  
- **Typical engagement duration:** 4–12 weeks for the main case; longer if technical design runs in parallel  
- **Typical team size:** 3–6 consultants plus SMEs / technical partners  
- **Primary consumer seniority:** manager / partner / board sponsor  
- **Decay risk:** medium  
- **Suggested owner discipline:** strategy and deals
