# Target operating model – design and activation blueprint

## When to use this skill

Use this when the strategy is clear enough and the client now needs the organization, governance, process, and capability design to execute it. Typical trigger phrases include “target operating model,” “org design,” “interaction model,” “delegation of authority,” “N-1 / N-2 structure,” and “activation plan.” Do **not** use it for a portfolio-level holding/subsidiary governance problem where the main question is group doctrine and subsidiary control; that is better handled by the holding-company governance skill.

## Deliverable shape

1. **Executive summary and model choice**
  Purpose: state the operating model answer and the design principles behind it.  
   Typical length: 2–3 slides.  
   Complete when: the chosen model, what it enables, and its main trade-offs are explicit.  
   Common failure modes: presenting org charts before the model logic, no design choices, no trade-offs.
2. **Mandate, scope, and design principles**
  Purpose: define what the operating model must enable and where the boundaries sit.  
   Typical length: 1–2 slides.  
   Complete when: mandate, scope, design principles, and key constraints are locked.  
   Common failure modes: mixing scope with structure, vague principles like “best practice” or “agile” with no meaning.
3. **Service / functional model**
  Purpose: define what work gets done and where it sits.  
   Typical length: 2–3 slides.  
   Complete when: core functions, service lines, or process families are grouped coherently and tied to mandate.  
   Common failure modes: copying existing departments, no clarity on what is centralized versus embedded.
4. **Governance and decision rights**
  Purpose: make control, challenge, and escalation practical.  
   Typical length: 2–3 slides.  
   Complete when: committees/boards/forums, decision rights, escalation thresholds, and reporting lines are explicit.  
   Common failure modes: forum-heavy governance with no real decisions mapped, duplicated approval layers, ambiguous ownership.
5. **Organization structure and roles**
  Purpose: show who will run the model.  
   Typical length: 2–4 slides.  
   Complete when: level 1 / level 2 structure, key roles, leadership accountabilities, and material spans & layers implications are visible.  
   Common failure modes: org chart with generic labels, no role differentiation, too much detail too early.
6. **Critical workflows / interaction model / RACI**
  Purpose: define how the model works day to day across units and stakeholders.  
   Typical length: 2–4 slides.  
   Complete when: 5–10 critical workflows, interfaces, handoffs, and RACI logic are described.  
   Common failure modes: process maps too detailed to read, no cross-functional interactions, workflows not linked to pain points.
7. **Capabilities, manpower, and enablers**
  Purpose: show the people, tools, data, and policies needed to make the model viable.  
   Typical length: 2–4 slides.  
   Complete when: critical skills, manpower ranges, technology/data enablers, and policy/process documentation needs are identified.  
   Common failure modes: manpower numbers with no logic, tech treated as a wishlist, capability gaps not prioritized.
8. **Performance management and control mechanisms**
  Purpose: embed accountability into the model.  
   Typical length: 1–2 slides.  
   Complete when: KPIs, scorecards, service levels, and review cadence are defined.  
   Common failure modes: KPI list without owners, no linkage to governance, purely activity metrics.
9. **Transition and activation plan**
  Purpose: move from target-state design to launch.  
   Typical length: 2–3 slides.  
   Complete when: sequencing, interim arrangements, documentation, handover, and change actions are specified.  
   Common failure modes: no interim model, no documents/toolkits, no day-1 versus target-state distinction.
10. **Appendix deliverables**

Purpose: capture the detailed operating artifacts needed for handover.  
Typical length: backup only.  
Complete when: committee ToRs, DoA, RACI matrices, process maps, JDs, manpower model, and toolkit references are listed.  
Common failure modes: critical details missing from appendix, or appendix replacing the main story.

## Visual framework library

- **Model choice page — TOM stack or house**: mandate and design principles at top, operating model levers underneath (governance, structure, processes, capabilities, technology, KPIs). Use this to explain the logic before showing the org chart.
- **Service model — activity clustering map**: cluster activities by function or service line, often by “strategy / business / delivery / enablement.” Use instead of legacy department labels.
- **Governance — layered forum map**: board / steering / management / working level with decision remit and cadence. Use when approval complexity is the problem; use a pure RACI only when the challenge is operational rather than governance.
- **Structure — level-1 org chart plus key role cards**: show top structure in the main deck and reserve level-2 detail for appendix unless the client specifically asked for it.
- **Interactions — interface heat map**: functions on both axes, color-coded by interaction intensity or decision dependency. Use before deep process swimlanes.
- **Critical workflow — swimlane with decision diamonds**: 5–9 steps max, focused on one problematic process (e.g., investment approval, budget cycle, asset activation, issue escalation).
- **Capabilities — capability heat map**: 5–7 critical capabilities × current / target maturity. Use when the client is underestimating the build needed.
- **Manpower — staffing wedge or role pyramid**: current vs target, by layer or function. Use when spans, layers, or setup cost matter.
- **Transition — day-1 / day-100 / target-state roadmap**: preferred when activation is phased or when interim consultant-run support is expected.

## House conventions

- **Action-title format**: structure pages as operational decisions, e.g., “A centralized strategy core with embedded delivery ownership best balances control and speed,” not “Target organization structure.”
- **Executive summary structure**: model answer, main design choices, headcount/capability implication, and activation path.
- **How findings are articulated**: current pain point → design implication → operating model choice. A TOM deck should show why each design decision exists.
- **Tone**: practical and adoption-oriented. This is not a theory deck.
- **Vocabulary**: “design principles,” “governance bodies,” “critical workflows,” “interaction model,” “DoA,” “shared services,” “CoE,” “embedded ownership,” “interim model,” “activation.”
- **Level of granularity**: show enough detail to operate, but do not drown the main deck in process manuals.

## Inputs the skill needs

- **Mandate and scope** (**Required**): what the entity/function/unit is expected to do.
- **Current-state structure/processes** (**Required**): org chart, governance, role definitions, major pain points, and process maps if available.
- **Strategy / business priorities** (**Required**): the TOM must enable something specific.
- **Stakeholder landscape** (**Required**): internal interfaces, parent entity, subsidiaries, regulators, vendors, or partner entities.
- **People and cost data** (**Optional but high value**): current manpower, spans, outsourcing, and key skill gaps.
- **Technology / data context** (**Optional but high value**): core systems, tooling constraints, reporting architecture.
- **Legal / policy constraints** (**Optional**): especially where committee design, DoA, or entity forms matter.
- **Change appetite / timing** (**Required**): whether the model is a clean-sheet future state, a near-term refresh, or a staged activation.

## Methodology anchors

- Start with mandate and critical workflows; org structure is downstream of both.
- Design decisions should be explicit on centralization, decision rights, and embedded versus shared ownership.
- Show a small number of critical workflows well; do not try to map every process in the main pack.
- Distinguish “who decides,” “who executes,” and “who is consulted.” Most TOM confusion comes from mixing these.
- Include interim arrangements when the target state cannot be staffed immediately.
- Treat KPI and governance design as part of the operating model, not separate work.
- Keep appendix artifacts reusable: committees, DoA, RACIs, and role cards are often what the client operationalizes first.

## Done-state checklist

- The model clearly enables the stated mandate and strategic priorities.
- Major centralization / decentralization choices are explicit.
- Governance bodies have real decisions assigned to them.
- The structure is understandable at a glance and does not require oral explanation.
- Critical workflows are mapped for the interfaces that matter most.
- Capabilities and manpower implications are credible and prioritized.
- KPIs, service levels, or control mechanisms are tied to owners and review cadence.
- The transition plan includes interim arrangements and documentation/toolkit outputs.
- There is no unresolved ambiguity on who owns what.
- The appendix is implementation-ready, not decorative.

## Composition notes

This skill commonly composes with **Corporate strategy – full strategic plan**, **Holding company / subsidiary governance and incorporation plan**, and **Kick-off, workplan and data request pack**. If the client asks for board/committee design across many subsidiaries, switch to the holding-company skill rather than forcing that complexity into a single-entity TOM.
